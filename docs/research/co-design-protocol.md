# AccessForge Co-design Research Protocol

Version: 0.1  
Status: planning only; no recruitment or data collection authorized  
Last updated: 2026-08-08

## 1. Purpose

This protocol defines how to learn whether AccessForge solves a real problem, whether its interaction burden is acceptable, and which safety boundaries are needed before implementation expands. It is a co-design activity, not a clinical study and not an attempt to diagnose or evaluate a participant’s body.

Do not begin recruitment, recording, or analysis until the project owner approves this protocol, consent wording, compensation, privacy controls, and a qualified reviewer confirms the plan is appropriate for the jurisdiction.

## 2. Questions

### Product questions

- What physical interactions are difficult but low-risk enough for a first template?
- How do participants currently solve the problem?
- Which measurements can participants provide comfortably and accurately?
- Which explanations help people trust or reject a design proposal?
- What makes a candidate feel personalized rather than generic?

### Safety questions

- Which apparently ordinary requests hide a safety-critical use?
- What language should trigger a professional-review or prohibited tier?
- What failure would make a participant stop using an adapter immediately?
- What testing evidence would a participant, helper, or professional expect before trying an output?

### Accessibility questions

- Can the workflow be completed without camera, audio, mouse, drag, two hands, or repeated movement?
- Which visual, audio, cognitive, and motor accommodations are needed?
- Are 3D previews understandable when paired with structured descriptions?

## 3. Sample

Target a total of 8–12 disabled participants with varied access needs, plus at least two relevant professionals such as an occupational therapist, rehabilitation engineer, accessibility practitioner, or experienced assistive-technology maker.

Recruitment must seek variation in:

- motor and dexterity needs
- use of assistive technology
- age and technology confidence
- experience with making or 3D printing
- hand dominance and one-handed/two-handed interaction
- communication preferences

Do not require participants to disclose a diagnosis. Ask only about the access goal and the interaction constraints necessary for the session.

Avoid tokenism: participants must be treated as paid contributors whose feedback can change scope, wording, design, or the decision to stop.

## 4. Recruitment and screening

Use accessible invitation text that states:

- the project is early-stage and may not produce a device
- participation is voluntary and unrelated to care or benefits
- the session may include discussing an everyday access difficulty
- the participant may decline any question or activity
- recording is optional and separate from participation
- payment amount, method, and timing
- contact details for questions and complaints

Do not recruit through a person’s clinician or employer in a way that could make refusal feel consequential. Screen for practical session needs, not medical eligibility.

## 5. Session format

Recommended 60–90 minute session:

1. Welcome, identity preference, communication/access check, and consent confirmation.
2. Explain what AccessForge can and cannot do.
3. Ask the participant to describe one ordinary interaction they would like to make easier.
4. Map the current attempt, workaround, desired outcome, and unacceptable failure.
5. Ask them to react to low-fidelity screens and sample DesignSpec/validation language.
6. If they choose, walk through a synthetic capture and measurement flow. Do not collect a real home video in Phase 0.
7. Ask what would make them trust, reject, or share a candidate.
8. Review what was recorded, allow corrections, and confirm deletion/retention choice.
9. Pay the participant and provide a plain-language summary/contact route.

Offer remote and in-person formats, text chat, captions, an interpreter/communication support person, breaks, and an early stop without penalty.

## 6. Research materials

Use synthetic sample projects for the first round:

- a pen grip thickener
- a zipper pull extender
- a cabinet handle sleeve

Do not use medical packaging, wheelchair parts, hot objects, electrical/gas controls, or anything that could make a participant feel pressured to demonstrate a risky action.

Prepare:

- plain-language information sheet
- consent form with separate checkboxes
- optional recording consent
- optional anonymized quote consent
- optional public/community contribution consent
- session script and accessible prototype screens
- compensation receipt
- deletion/withdrawal log
- incident/escalation contact sheet

## 7. Consent and privacy

Consent is a process, not a signature. Reconfirm before any optional recording or change of activity. The participant can withdraw without explaining why.

Collect the minimum information necessary. Do not store names, contact details, raw recordings, or transcripts in this repository. Store research materials in an approved access-controlled location outside the product database. Use a participant code in notes, and keep the re-identification key separate with restricted access.

If a participant describes a safety-critical need, do not design the part in the session. Record the concern only with permission, explain the boundary, and refer to qualified help as appropriate.

## 8. Compensation

Pay for time and expertise, not for positive feedback or successful output. Set the amount before recruitment, make it independent of whether the participant completes the entire session, and reimburse agreed accessibility costs. Obtain appropriate tax/legal advice for the jurisdiction.

The consent document must state the payment, payment timing, cancellation policy, and whether payment information is stored separately from research notes.

## 9. Data handling

Classify all research materials as restricted. Use encrypted storage, least-privilege access, a retention date, and a deletion owner. Do not use research content to train models or publish it without separate consent.

Before retaining any quote or screenshot:

- remove identifying details
- ask whether the participant approves the intended context
- preserve their preferred name/pronouns or remain anonymous as requested
- allow later withdrawal where feasible

## 10. Analysis

Use a structured synthesis table with columns for:

- access goal
- current workaround
- interaction constraints
- unacceptable failure
- measurement burden
- preferred language
- trust signal
- privacy concern
- accessibility need
- proposed product change
- evidence strength

Separate participant observation from researcher interpretation. Have at least two people independently review a subset of notes, then resolve differences. Do not publish small-cell demographic details that could identify someone.

## 11. Stop rules

Pause the study and consult the owner/safety reviewer if:

- a participant reports pain, injury, distress, or unsafe pressure to continue
- the session reveals a use case outside the MVP that the team cannot explain safely
- recording or storage consent is unclear
- a participant’s data is exposed to an unauthorized person or service
- the team cannot provide the promised accessibility accommodation

## 12. Phase 0 success criteria

Phase 0 research planning is successful when:

- at least eight participant slots and two professional-review slots can be recruited without coercion
- the script surfaces unacceptable failure and privacy expectations
- the team can explain the MVP in plain language
- every prototype task has a non-camera and non-audio alternative
- findings can change the MVP scope and template order
- the owner has approved the protocol and consent before any participant data is collected
